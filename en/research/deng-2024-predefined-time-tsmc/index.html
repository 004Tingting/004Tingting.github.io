Booking the Settling Time with Delayed Feedback: Notes on Deng et al. (2024)

Oct 12, 2026

Yang Deng, Emmanuel Moulay, Vincent Léchappé, Zhang Chen, Bin Liang, Franck Plestan. Robust Nonsingular Predefined-Time Terminal Sliding Mode Control for Perturbed Chains of Integrators. IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control, 69(12): 8946–8953, 2024. DOI: 10.1109/TAC.2024.3426554; author copy at hal.science/hal-04637935. Affiliations: Department of Automation, Tsinghua University (Deng, Chen, Liang; Chen corresponding), XLIM, Université de Poitiers (Moulay), Ampère, INSA Lyon (Léchappé), LS2N, Nantes Université (Plestan). Supported by NSFC grants 62203252 and 62073183.

1. What the paper is after

Terminal sliding mode control (TSMC) is usually credited with two things: invariance to matched disturbances once on the sliding surface, and finite-time convergence. The field has moved past "finite" to fixed-time: a settling-time bound independent of initial conditions (Polyakov, 2012). But fixed-time TSMC carries an old defect, singularity: on classical terminal sliding surfaces the control blows up as the state approaches the origin. The existing nonsingular fixed-time TSMC literature (a long line starting with Zuo, 2015) has two common weaknesses:

  1. singularity is handled by switching, either between sliding surfaces or between control laws;
  2. almost all of it covers double integrators or second-order systems only; high-order chains of integrators are left out.

Pal et al. (2022) and Chen et al. (2022) used time scaling and variable gains to avoid switching altogether, delivering nonsingular predefined-time TSMC, but only for matched disturbances. A separate line is the smooth periodic delayed feedback of Zhou, Michiels and Chen (2021): artificial delays buy fixed-time convergence in a smooth form, at the cost of the robustness defect stated in their Remark 4, namely that controllers built that way do not preserve robust predefined-time stability under perturbations or model uncertainties.

This paper welds the two lines together: keep the form of delayed feedback for the sliding surface, and leave robustness to sliding mode theory. The resulting control law is nonsingular and switching-free; it covers matched disturbances and mismatched ones; and it extends from double integrators all the way to n-th order chains of integrators.

Terminology first. Predefined-time means the user picks the settling instant T_c up front and tunes the controller for it; convergence happens no later than T_c. Fixed-time only promises a bound independent of initial conditions, indirectly set by parameters. The paper also uses a stricter notion, all nonzero initial conditions converging exactly at T_c, which it calls prescribed-time; I keep the English.

2. The core idea: encode the settling instant into when the gain switches on

The building block is a scalar system with an artificial delay (Lemma 3):

ζ˙(t)=a ζ(t)−K(t) ζ(t−h)\dot{\zeta}(t) = a\,\zeta(t) - K(t)\,\zeta(t-h)ζ˙​(t)=aζ(t)−K(t)ζ(t−h)

where R(t)R(t)R(t) is a smooth bump on [h,2h][h, 2h][h,2h] (M>1M > 1M>1; shape schematic, zero at endpoints):

The normalising factor is computed offline from aaa and is always positive:

W=∫h2hR(θ) e−2aθ dθW = \int_{h}^{2h} R(\theta)\,e^{-2a\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\thetaW=∫h2h​R(θ)e−2aθdθ

Claim: for any ζ(0) ≠ 0, ζ(t) hits zero exactly at t = 2h, and stays there since K ≡ 0 afterwards.

The proof skeleton is worth walking through, because it shows where "exactly at the instant" comes from:

  • on [0,h)[0, h)[0,h): K≡0K \equiv 0K≡0, the system degenerates to the linear ζ′=a ζ\zeta' = a\,\zetaζ′=aζ, with solution ζ(t)=eat ζ(0)\zeta(t) = e^{at}\,\zeta(0)ζ(t)=eatζ(0);
  • on [h,2h][h, 2h][h,2h]: the delayed term is active and the solution takes the form ζ(t)=ea(t−h)[1−1W∫htR(θ) e−2aθ dθ] W ζ(0)\zeta(t) = e^{a(t-h)}\Big[1 - \frac{1}{W}\int_{h}^{t} R(\theta)\,e^{-2a\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\Big]\,W\,\zeta(0)ζ(t)=ea(t−h)[1−W1​∫ht​R(θ)e−2aθdθ]Wζ(0). The integrand is positive, the integral increases monotonically, and by the definition of WWW it reaches exactly 1, so the bracket climbs from 0 to 1 and hits 0 at t=2ht = 2ht=2h;
  • for t ≥ 2h: K ≡ 0 again, ζ' = a·ζ with ζ(2h) = 0, and the origin is kept.

Three observations:

  1. the exact instant is manufactured by the normalising integral W, not approached asymptotically; that is the essence of predefined-time as opposed to fixed-time;
  2. the claim holds for any a ∈ R; the sign of a shapes the transient but not the settling instant;
  3. the delay h is a tuning parameter of the sliding surface, not a physical delay of the plant, and the paper says so explicitly. Implementation needs a buffer of state history over [0, 2h], but h is a small design parameter (0.5 s and 0.75 s in the simulations), so the cost is negligible.

Where does robustness come from? The key move is that the delayed feedback lives only in the sliding surface. A sliding surface is a chosen geometry, unaffected by perturbations and independent of the model; disturbances can only enter through the reaching phase, which is handled by the robust fixed-time term of Moulay et al. (2022). The duties are split: the surface decides when, the reaching term makes sure it gets there despite disturbances. The robustness defect of Zhou et al.'s delayed feedback is sidestepped this way.

For a practical setting like CAV control, this is a more honest conclusion than "converges to the origin".

5. Assessment and things to verify

What it does well:

  • nonsingular without switching; the surface is smooth throughout and the control is bounded;
  • one structural step from double integrators to n-th order chains, via cascaded surfaces and shifted gain windows;
  • matched and mismatched disturbances both covered, the latter with its own observer bank;
  • "delayed feedback only inside the surface" simultaneously buys predefined time and dodges the robustness defect of delayed feedback;
  • the settling instant becomes an explicit design parameter: Tc=(2n−1) hT_c = (2n-1)\,hTc​=(2n−1)h (2n h2n\,h2nh with mismatched disturbances); shrink hhh to converge sooner.

Costs and limitations, from my reading, to be checked in reproduction:

  • the control needs derivatives of the sliding variables up to order n−1n-1n−1 (clean state measurement) and derivatives of the gains (computable offline);
  • the artificial delay requires a buffer of hhh seconds of state history; smaller hhh shortens the buffer but enlarges K˙′\dot{K}'K˙′ inside the control law, so tuning has to be watched;
  • choosing aaa is delicate: Proposition 1's aaa depends on initial conditions, i.e. open-loop tuning, while Remark 3 asks for a sign switch after convergence; how to do that smoothly in closed loop is left open;
  • under persistent disturbance, a continuous reaching term means a boundary layer near the surface rather than exact invariance; the paper's "s≡0s \equiv 0s≡0 for t≥3ht \ge 3ht≥3h" is an ideal sliding-mode statement;
  • the disturbance assumptions are not light: bounded matched disturbance, and bounded derivatives for the mismatched ones;
  • simulations only; experimental validation and nonlinear triangular systems are listed as future work, and CAV longitudinal dynamics is precisely triangular, so one step remains before direct use.

On CAV relevance: longitudinal collision avoidance and platooning often reduce the relative dynamics to a double or triple integrator (position, velocity, acceleration chain, plus unknown drag and mass perturbations), and predefined-time convergence turns "when does the platoon re-converge" into a design quantity, which fits deadline-driven scenarios such as merge conflict zones and green-light windows better than fixed-time's "there is a bound anyway". The smallest step toward my own topic: reproduce the mismatched triple-integrator case, replace d with speed-dependent drag, and check whether the Tc=6hT_c = 6hTc​=6h tuning and the TO≤hT_O \le hTO​≤h constraint still hold at a realistic sampling rate.
